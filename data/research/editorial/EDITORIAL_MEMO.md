# Editorial memo: narrative threads worth telling

Prepared 2026-08-19. Source base: 42 reading logs in `data/research/editorial/readings/`. Output: 81 dated events in `events.json`, 13 threads in `stories.json`.

Every claim below traces to a reading log. Where logs disagree, the conflict is stated.

## What the reading base actually is

The 42 logs are not evenly spread. Ten of them are full issues of two Newark weeklies from 1938, 1939, and 1942. Nine are short clippings about three 19th-century figures. The rest are single-item mentions from mainstream New Jersey dailies, plus five cover scans and PDFs of later magazines. So the archive can tell deep stories about Newark in 1938 and about three men in the 1880s and 1890s. It can only gesture at everything between 1942 and 1990.

Two logs sit outside that pattern. The MEDIC News launch issue of April 1971 and the January 7, 2022 issue of The Nubian News are the only full issues read from after 1942. The Nubian News issue is the only firsthand read of a paper that is still publishing. It carries a full masthead, 26 named people, and eight dated incidents, which makes it the single best evidence in the base for what a contemporary New Jersey Black paper actually prints. One issue is not a thread, so it does not appear in the ranking below.

## Ranked threads

### 1. The one-man newspaper of Saddle River

Alfred P. Smith wrote, edited, printed, translated, and delivered The Landscape by himself for twenty years. He wrote to Lincoln in 1862 against colonizing freed people. He editorialized against Southern lynching and against Englewood's segregated schools. He won a road-paving vote. The paper died when he did.

Evidence: three reading logs, all retrospectives (Ridgewood News 1987, The Record 1991, Sunday News 1993). All three name him, the paper, and the 1901 end.

Missing: no original issue of The Landscape has been read. Microfilm is at the Upper Saddle River Library per the 1993 piece. The three accounts conflict on the start date (May 1881 in two, June 1882 in one) and on his age at death (65 vs 69) and the death date (July 1901 vs four months after July 1901). Resolve before publishing a date on the site. Also unread: David Steven Cohen's essay, cited as the source for the 1987 piece.

Why it leads: one named person, a clear arc, a full run of dates, and a face. The 1987 piece includes a photo portrait.

### 2. R. Henri Herbert, and the family behind him

Herbert founded The Weekly Sentinel in Trenton in 1880, sold it after three years, and turned the same organizing method on Republican politics. In 1893 he circulated a letter telling Black Monmouth County voters to defeat Asbury Park founder James A. Bradley over the pavilion color line. In 1895 the state committee made him its consulting member for the Black vote. In 1909 he dropped dead in a Trenton street and the New York Age gave him page one.

His father, Mansfield Herbert, escaped slavery at 14, bought freedom for himself, his father, and his sister for over $3,000, and ran the principal Underground Railroad station between Philadelphia and New Brunswick from 1843 to the Civil War.

Evidence: four reading logs (New-York Tribune 1895, Trenton Sunday Advertiser 1893 and 1895, New York Age 1909).

Missing: no issue of The Weekly Sentinel has been read. The 1895 Tribune profile is the only source for the father's story and for Herbert's earlier newspaper jobs, so it is single-sourced. Colonel E. C. Stahl and The Trenton Herald are unresearched.

Why it ranks high: it connects the press to the Underground Railroad and to electoral power in one family, and it is the archive's earliest documented NJ Black newspaper founding.

### 3. Colonel Murrell, the Trumpet, and Asbury Park

Col. William Murrell edited the New Jersey Trumpet in Newark. In July 1893 he spoke at the Marrow's Hall mass meeting against Bradley's signs barring Black beachgoers. Within a week the Shore Press noted that the first issue of a Trumpet Monmouth edition, edited locally by Rev. Fillmore Smith, had reached its office. The launch date is not stated in any log, only the July 28 notice. The paper chased the story into the county.

Evidence: four logs (Washington Bee 1889, Asbury Park Daily Press and Shore Press 1893, Evening World 1888).

Conflicts and gaps: the two 1893 accounts of the same meeting put attendance at about 100 and about 500. The 1889 Washington Bee said it would take over the Trumpet's subscribers, implying the Trumpet was ending, but the paper was still publishing in 1893. The 1888 Evening World squib is illegible, so the Trumpet link there is unconfirmed. No issue of the Trumpet has been read.

### 4. Two Newark weeklies become one

In 1932 the Afro-American reported the Newark Herald had folded after four years. By 1938 the Herald was at Volume 11 while a new rival, the New Jersey Herald News, was at Volume 1 with the motto "Owned and Published by New Jersey Negroes." A single archive scan holds both papers for June 11, 1938. By March 1939 the Herald News masthead read "combined with THE NEWARK HERALD" and claimed to be the state's oldest colored weekly in its twelfth year. By 1942 it printed from 130 W. Kinney St., the Herald's old address.

Evidence: seven logs (Afro-American 1932, five 1938 Newark Herald scans, Herald News 1939 and 1942).

Conflicts to resolve before publishing: the 1932 folding report cannot be squared with Volume 11 numbering in 1938 without an explanation. The Herald News jumps from Volume 1 in 1938 to Volume XII in 1939, which means it absorbed the Herald's volume count when it absorbed the paper. Masthead names differ between issues in the same month: Merrill Biddle, Julius X. Adams, and F. Sweeney Jones on June 11, 1938, but Dr. R. E. Bell, Fulton J. Adams, and B. Sweeney Jones on July 9, 1938. Melvin B. Johnson in one issue and Melvin R. Johnson in another. Oliver W. Brown and Olive W. Brown. Treat these as OCR variance until a clean scan settles them. The June 11, 1938 log is the outlier on two of these: it alone reads "Julius X. Adams" and "Melvin R. Johnson," against seven logs reading Julius J. Adams and Melvin B. Johnson, so the events and stories files use the majority spellings. The publication file also carries the Newark Herald twice, as ids 9 and 24, with different end years (1939 and 1999).

### 5. A civil rights beat before the civil rights movement

Across 1938 to 1942 the Newark weeklies ran a continuous local civil rights beat: a Jim Crow theater suit in Elizabeth, a Matawan landlord barring Black guests, a Newark bar refusing service to Urban League staff, a Long Branch beach ordinance, an Elizabeth pool that closed rather than integrate, a Klan cross-burning near Butler, a state commission finding 200,000 Black residents denied their rights, and a Rutgers admissions inquiry the paper called a whitewash. In March 1939 it turned the beat into a planned municipal investigative series, city by city.

Evidence: five full-issue logs.

Missing: only eight issues across five years have been read. The promised Jersey City installment of the 1939 series has not been located. Nothing between 1942 and 1966, the end year in the publication file.

Why it matters for the site: this is the clearest argument that the NJ Black press was doing accountability journalism decades before the movement it is usually credited with covering.

### 6. Melvin B. Johnson leaves the press for the governor's office

Johnson was managing editor of the New Jersey Herald News in May 1938. He left the Black newspaper business in 1946 to organize Black voters for Alfred Driscoll. By 1949 he advised the governor on minority-group affairs, worked in the state Labor Department, and was a prospective Newark City Commission candidate.

Evidence: three logs (Herald News 1938 masthead, Star-Ledger 1949, Asbury Park Press 1949).

Missing: the 1949 clippings say he edited and published weeklies in Montclair and Newark. The archive attributes no Montclair paper to him. Find it. Note the Melvin B. / Melvin R. spelling conflict between the two 1938 mastheads.

### 7. The Echo survives a fire and moves down the shore

The Echo began in Long Branch under editors Rock and Howard. A suspected arson fire gutted its press room in 1904. It kept publishing. By 1909 editor W. E. Rack moved it to Red Bank. By 1921 the New York Age was reprinting Red Bank Echo items alongside the Detroit Leader and the Christian Recorder.

Evidence: three logs (Monmouth Democrat 1904, Asbury Park Press 1909, New York Age 1921).

Conflicts: "Rock" in 1904 and "Rack" in 1909 are probably the same person, spelled two ways. The publication file gives 1904 as the founding year, but the 1904 fire item refers to the paper's first issue in the past tense, so the founding is earlier.

### 8. Printers who became publishers

Two Black New Jersey publishing operations start with a press, not a newsroom, ninety years apart. Smith opened a job-printing shop in 1876 and started a paper partly to advertise it. MEDIC Enterprises in Newark founded MEDIC Press in July 1969 with $9,000 of donated letterpress equipment, then launched MEDIC News in April 1971. Editor Carolyn Odom wrote that the newsletter was heir to "the black journals of the early 1800's."

Evidence: two logs, but the MEDIC log is unusually rich. It names 39 people and a 29-member board including LeRoi Jones and Wilbur Parker.

Missing: only one issue of MEDIC News has been read, and it is issue one. Whether it ran past 1971 is unknown. TRENDCO in Trenton and BPUM in Camden are named as sibling organizations funded under the same grant and may have had their own newsletters.

### 9. Bordentown's student paper wins on the national stage

The Ironsides Echo, monthly paper of the state's Manual Training and Industrial School for Colored Youth, placed second in Columbia University's scholastic press contest in 1932 against 800 publications, then took two more second places in 1940. The same campus hosted the NJ State Federation of Colored Women's Clubs conventions the Newark weeklies covered.

Evidence: two logs (Courier-Post 1932, Trenton Times 1940), plus 1938 Newark coverage of the campus.

Missing: no issue of the Ironsides Echo has been read. Student editor Leon Snead is named once and never again.

### 10. The women's club movement on the front page

In July 1938 the New Jersey Herald News, then still printing as the Newark Herald, covered the state Federation of Colored Women's Clubs convention at Bordentown as hard news. It front-paged a WPA official's remark about "Mammy" and Mrs. Armeta H. Douglass's objection to it, then reported that Mrs. Alice B. Arrington resigned the presidency for a YWCA post in Baltimore and that Mrs. Ida Brown succeeded her, with founder Rev. Florence Randolph presiding. Randolph reappears in 1942 marking 45 years in the ministry. All the 1938 and 1942 sources here are that one title, publication id 16.

Evidence: three 1938 issue logs plus the 1942 issue.

Missing: nothing on the Federation before or after 1938 in this batch. Three Black women are documented in this batch in editorial roles: Lillian B. Huff, society editor of the Newark Herald in 1938; Carolyn Odom at MEDIC News in 1971; and Tanya Henry Turner, managing editor of The Nubian News in 2022. None is documented as a publisher. That gap is worth naming on the site rather than hiding.

### 11. Newark's byline culture

The 1938 papers name their own staff constantly: Julius J. Adams on "Sophisticates," Lou Blackmon on "Mainly About Tan Town," Frank Tucker on "Sportbeams," Charles Countee Edwards on "The New Jersey Whirl Uncensored," Lillian B. Huff as society editor, William H. A. Moore on "Judge Moore This Week." Harry B. Webber, city editor of the Herald News, later published his own Newark magazine, After Hours, and wrote "Jersey Happenings" for the Afro-American until he died in 1991 at 90.

Evidence: four 1938 issue logs plus the 1991 Courier-News obituary.

Missing: Webber is the only one of these people followed past 1938. Every other name is a dead end in this batch. Obituary searches on Adams, Blackmon, Huff, and Tucker would likely turn each into a small profile.

### 12. Utimme Umana/La Voz Oculta outlasts the campus

In April 1976 Trenton State College trustees voted to arm campus police. The minority publication opposed it alongside the mainstream student paper. Seventeen years later the college still listed it among about twenty minority organizations, now called a magazine.

Evidence: two logs (Trenton Times 1976 and 1993).

Missing: no issue read. The name changes between the two clippings ("Utimme/La Voz Oculta" then "Utimme Umana/La Voz Oculta") and the format changes from weekly to magazine. The College of New Jersey archives are the obvious next stop.

### 13. Two Black consumer magazines, documented only by cover artifacts

Marked weak in `stories.json`. Two consumer titles appear in the reading base as artifacts only. Word Up! is a Sotheby's auction photo of a stack of issues headed by an August premiere issue fronting Run-DMC. Rap Masters is a single November 1992 cover fronting Kris Kross.

Evidence: two logs, and both are weak. Neither is an editorial page. Neither shows a New Jersey imprint, a masthead, or an office address.

Do not lead with this, and do not repeat the earlier framing that publishing's center of gravity moved from Newark weeklies to Bergen County magazine offices. No log supports that geography or that causal claim; it has been removed from `stories.json`. The publication record puts these titles in Paramus, but the artifacts read so far do not confirm it. Get a masthead page before making the claim on the site.

## Conflicts to settle before anything is published

1. The Landscape start year: 1881 or 1882. Smith's age at death: 65 or 69. Death month: July or November 1901.
2. Newark Herald: reported folded in 1932, publishing at Volume 11 in 1938. Also duplicated in the publication file as ids 9 and 24 with end years 1939 and 1999.
3. Newark Herald mastheads name different officers in June and July 1938.
4. New Jersey Herald News editor: Melvin B. Johnson or Melvin R. Johnson. Business manager: Oliver W. Brown or Olive W. Brown.
5. The Echo editor: Rock or Rack. Founding year predates the 1904 listed in the publication file.
6. New Jersey Trumpet: the 1889 Washington Bee implies it was ending; it was still publishing in 1893.
7. Asbury Park meeting attendance: about 100 or about 500.
8. The Nubian News frequency: the publication file says monthly, and the Danky 1998 catalog entry says monthly, but the January 7, 2022 issue reads as a weekly.
9. The Nubian News volume count: the publication file gives a founding of circa 1988-1989, but the January 7, 2022 issue is numbered Volume 6, No. 1. Either the paper restarted its volume count or the founding year is wrong. Do not print a continuous-run claim until this is settled.

## Known dead ends in this batch

The Captain Africa cover thumbnail carries no date and no New Jersey marking. The 1936 Camden CCC camp article does not state the company's racial makeup, so the expected camp-paper link is unconfirmed. The 1888 Evening World squib is illegible. Three of the 42 logs yielded nothing usable. The Nubian News 2022 PDF was previously listed here as unopenable; it has since been read in full and is the source for events 71 through 79.
